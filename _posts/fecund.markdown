What is the purest form of technology?

With software, the goal is to reform the previous paradigm. When building with AI, a strong heuristic for success is to achieve escape velocity over the interface that came before. 1. it writes code >100x faster than a human (current upper bound is Gemini Diffusion, which writes code [over 1000x faster](https://wandb.ai/byyoung3/ml-news/reports/Google-DeepMind-unveils-Gemini-Diffusion-LLM--VmlldzoxMjkwMDkyNQ) than [humans](https://sambanova.ai/blog/tokens-per-second-is-not-all-you-need)), and

## Superbioligical Race Dynamics

Shortly after DeepBlue beat Garry Kasparov at chess in 1997, astrophysicist Dr. Piet Hut made a prediction published in the [New York Times](https://www.nytimes.com/1997/07/29/science/to-test-a-powerful-computer-play-an-ancient-game.html): *“It may be a hundred years before a computer beats humans at Go — maybe even longer.”* The justification was intruiging: *“If a reasonably intelligent person learned to play Go, in a few months he could beat all existing computer programs. You don’t have to be a Kasparov.”*

The rationale seemed robust; Go is a game infinitely more computationally complex, with a much larger game space of 10^700 versus chess’ 10^120, and a branching factor of 250 versus chess' 35. The argument was a relatively one (versus an ordinal one [1]); the heuristics learned by the best Go players approximated a game space of 10^700 moves versus chess’ 10^120, and a branching factor of 250 versus chess' 35. As a rule, the more ancient the game, the more combined work performed to develop the heuristics at the state of the art, hence "to test a powerful computer, play an ancient game". 

Of course, just 19 years later, AlphaGo became the first machine to beat the human benchmark at Go, outpacing Hut's prediction by a factor of five. But did the second, implied prediction play out? If so, we should see a clear line between the "AlphaGo moment" and the beating of all computer programmes.

Let's plot the aftermath:

Eighteen months after AlphaGo, DeepMind released AlphaGo Zero, a system that mastered the ancient game of Go with nothing but the rules; doing away with any human game data or handcrafted features—and within three days, it surpassed AlphaGo Lee. By 40 days, it dominated all previous iterations, playing at levels no human or algorithm could match. AlphaZero searched just 80,000 positions per second compared to Stockfish's 70 million, yet won decisively. 

A few months later, AlphaZero was released, capable of dominating chess, shogi, and Go simultaneously after mere *hours* of self-play.

A year after that, they released MuZero, dispensing any explicit rules and learning game dynamics purely via interaction. This paradigm, which is still being developed, is designed to let algorithms study games where no simulator can be developed, and outperform all symbolic algorithms (those defined by rule-based logic, rather than MuZero-style subsymbolic reasoning). At the time, MuZero scored high or unprecedented points in every Atari game just by looking at the screen and playing by itself.

It has been five years since MuZero, and we have experienced the entire generative algorithm arc, and now games like Minecraft and Pokemon are being won by algorithms that beat MuZero at Atari, using language models to greatly improve sample efficiency. For a growing number of games, these systems now benchmark only against themselves, leaving human performance horizons behind, and vindicating Dr. Hut.

Black Holes as Ultimate Computational Boundaries
- Black holes represent the most extreme computational boundaries in our universe for several reasons:

- Bekenstein Bound: This fundamental limit, derived by Jacob Bekenstein, states that any physical system contained in a finite region of space has a maximum information content. For black holes, this maximum is proportional to the surface area of the event horizon (in Planck units), not the volume.

- Holographic Principle: This principle suggests that all the information contained in a volume of space can be represented as information on the boundary of that region. Black holes are the most perfect manifestation of this principle - all their information is encoded on their event horizons.

- Information Processing Rate: Seth Lloyd and others have proposed that black holes may represent the ultimate limit to information processing speed, with a maximum computational density determined by their mass and radius.

Information Sequestration Mechanics
When information "falls" into a black hole, several profound things happen:

- Apparent Information Loss: From an outside observer's perspective, the information becomes inaccessible, though not immediately "lost." This creates a tension with quantum mechanics' requirement that information must be conserved.

- Event Horizon Encoding: According to some theories, information that falls into a black hole becomes encoded on the event horizon through subtle quantum correlations. The information is effectively "frozen" at the horizon from an external observer's viewpoint.

- Scrambling: Black holes are thought to be nature's fastest scramblers of information. Information that falls in becomes maximally mixed and encrypted in a time proportional to the black hole radius times logarithm of mass (in natural units).

- Computational Irretrievability: The information inside a black hole isn't just physically inaccessible but computationally scrambled in a way that may represent a fundamental irreversibility beyond thermodynamic irreversibility.

- QECI (Quantum Extended Church-Turing) Thesis: This conjecture suggests that physical reality cannot efficiently solve problems beyond the reach of quantum computers. Black holes may represent the boundary case where this thesis is tested.

- Complexity Classes: The difficulty of retrieving information from a black hole might be related to complexity classes in computer science. Some problems may be "trapped" in the black hole in a way analogous to problems in the computational complexity class PSPACE.

Size and Fundamental Properties
Black holes are characterized primarily by three parameters:

Mass: Determines gravitational influence and event horizon size
Spin: Affects spacetime dragging and ergosphere properties
Charge: Rare in astrophysical black holes but theoretically possible

The Schwarzschild radius (event horizon) scales linearly with mass: R = 2GM/c². A solar-mass black hole has a radius of about 3 kilometers.
Hawking Radiation Relationship
The inverse relationship between black hole size and temperature is one of their most counterintuitive properties:

Temperature: T ∝ 1/M (inversely proportional to mass)
Power output: P ∝ 1/M² (inversely proportional to mass squared)
Lifespan: τ ∝ M³ (proportional to mass cubed)

Smaller black holes are hotter and radiate energy more intensely, leading to faster evaporation.

Size Categories and Properties
Primordial/Micro Black Holes (< 10¹⁵ kg)

Event horizon smaller than an atom
Temperature potentially millions of degrees
Significant Hawking radiation
Extremely short lifetimes (those below 10¹¹ kg would have already evaporated)
Minimum theoretical size limited by quantum gravity (near Planck mass ~10⁻⁸ kg)

Stellar Black Holes (10³⁰-10³² kg)

~10-100 km event horizon
Temperature ~10⁻⁸ K (effectively cold)
Negligible Hawking radiation
Evaporation time exceeds 10⁶⁷ years
Strong tidal forces near horizon

Supermassive Black Holes (10³⁶-10⁴² kg)

Millions to billions of km horizon
Ultra-cold temperature
Virtually no measurable Hawking radiation
Gentle tidal forces at the horizon
Evaporation time exceeds the age of the universe by many orders of magnitude

Technological Implications for Civilization
Advantages of Small Black Holes

Energy Production: A civilization that could create and contain micro black holes might harness their Hawking radiation as an extraordinarily efficient power source
Waste Disposal: Perfect matter-to-energy conversion for hazardous materials
Computing: Theoretical use in quantum information processing, potentially exploiting holographic encoding on the event horizon
Propulsion: Could serve as the basis for extremely efficient spacecraft engines

Disadvantages of Small Black Holes

Containment Challenges: Extremely difficult to contain due to their high energy output
Catastrophic Risk: Uncontrolled micro black holes could potentially consume matter in unpredictable ways
Short Lifespans: Rapid evaporation culminating in energetic explosions
Gravitational Instability: Challenging to maintain in stable positions

Minimum Possible Size
The theoretical minimum size for a black hole is approximately the Planck mass (~10⁻⁸ kg, with a radius of ~10⁻³⁵ meters), though creating such objects would require energy concentrations far beyond current or near-future technology.
Black holes smaller than ~10¹¹ kg would have already completely evaporated through Hawking radiation during the current age of the universe.

Such black holes would have lifespans of thousands to millions of years and radiation temperatures potentially usable for energy generation

Black Hole Lifespans Based on Initial Mass
For a black hole that forms and then never consumes additional matter, its lifespan is entirely determined by its initial mass due to Hawking radiation. Here's how long these isolated black holes would exist before completely evaporating:
Mathematical Relationship
The evaporation time can be calculated using:
τ ≈ 5,120π G² M³/(ħc⁴)
Which simplifies to the approximation:
τ ≈ 8.4 × 10⁶⁶ years × (M/M☉)³
Where M☉ is one solar mass (2 × 10³⁰ kg).

Key Observations

Scale Factor: Note the cubic relationship—a black hole with 10× the mass lasts 1,000× longer.
Universe Age Threshold: Black holes with mass greater than ~10¹¹ kg (about the mass of a small moon) would outlive the current age of the universe.
Stellar Black Holes: A typical stellar black hole of 10 solar masses would last approximately 8.4 × 10⁶⁹ years, vastly exceeding the projected lifespan of stars and galaxies.
Supermassive Black Holes: The largest known black holes (~10⁴⁰ kg) would evaporate over timescales so vast (~10⁹⁶ years) that they exceed most other projected cosmic processes by many orders of magnitude.
