### <a id="part-1"></a>The Status Quo

The conventional understanding of black holes emerged in the 1960s when Martin Kruskal provided a complete description of the Schwarzschild solution to Einstein's equations. In this picture, a black hole forms where matter collapses to a point of infinite density, creating an event horizon through which nothing can pass.

The view is: 
- The horizon itself is smooth and unremarkable to an observer crossing it
- Inside the horizon lies a region containing a curvature singularity where Einstein's equations break down
- Matter that crosses the horizon inevitably encounters this singularity; space and time essentially flip

The problem with this view is what is known as the "Information Paradox", arising when information falls into the black hole and appears to be irretrivably lost when the black hole disappears due to Hawking radiation. This violates the principle of unitarity in quantum mechanics. A derivative issue is known as the "Firewall Paradox" which argues that in order to preserve quantum information, we'd need to replace the event horizon with a highly energetic firewall that would incinerate anything crossing it. The problem is that for this to hold, the principle of equivalence would be violated. 

Beyond all this, there's a more profound and interesting notion of "The Singularity Problem", which is why I'm really interested in this research. If we pass through the singularity, we'd encounter a region of spacetime where the laws of physics break down, and to be clear: this is a skill issue. As of April 2025, something profound is missing about our understanding of black holes.

### <a id="part-2"></a>The Black Mirror Hypothesis

This is Turok, Boyle and Tzanavaris' attempt to resolve Einstein's equations with an alternative view of black holes. 

The view is that singularities and event horizons are wrongfully treated as distinct ideas. The argument is that at the event horizon, something profound happens, where exterior spacetime is not merely connected to an interior with a singularity, but directly to its own image, which is known as Charge, Parity, and Time (CPT) reversal symmetry. Matter approaching the horizon from one side meets its antimatter counterpart approaching from the mirror side, meaning all curvature invariants are finite, eigenvalues just "swap places" exactly at the horizon, meaning they do not violate the principle of equivalence, for information undergoes transformation. 

The annihilation event means that matter cannot cross the horizon intact, but unitarity is maintained as the annihilation produces radiation that travels along the horizon and eventually escapes as Hawking radiation.- any global charge entering from one side is canceled by the corresponding anti-charge entering from the mirror side, allowing global symmetries to remain exact—consistent with current expectations that symmetries like B-L (baryon minus lepton number) are conserved.

In quantum mechanics, a particle can be in multiple states at once. This is called superposition. Once you measure it, the state collapses into a single “eigenstate.” A common example is that particles can be in a combination of different energy states at the same time, and when you measure their energy, you get one of those possible values. After that, the particle is stuck in that energy state, at least until something else interacts with it. As far as we know, this randomness isn’t because we’re missing information, but because it’s inherent in nature. The theory assumes that measuring something (which really just means interacting with it in some way, like bouncing a photon off it) is what causes the collapse. Some alternative ideas, called hidden variable theories, suggest that particles do have definite states all along and we just can’t see them, but those aren’t widely accepted.

In quantum field theory, which is a generalization of quantum mechanics that includes special relativity, particles are thought of as little ripples in invisible fields that fill all of space and time. One of the consequences of this theory is that most particles have a corresponding antiparticle, basically a twin with the same mass but opposite quantum numbers, like electric charge.

There’s a phenomenon called meson mixing, where certain particles called mesons can actually turn into their own antiparticles over time. This only happens with neutral mesons, because flipping charge would otherwise break charge conservation. For the charm meson, it is made up of a charm quark and an anti-up quark. Its antiparticle has an anti-charm quark and an up quark. These two are different in terms of quark content, but due to the superposition principle they can turn into each other. This is because the states that have definite mass, which are the ones that actually evolve in time, are combinations of the flavor states (like charm and up). So if I measure the mass of the meson, it collapses into a mass state that’s made of both flavor states. But then if I go and check its flavor, it collapses into one of the flavor states again, which is then a combination of the mass states. So we can’t ever pin down both perfectly. This back-and-forth is what causes the meson to sometimes look like its antiparticle, even though it started out different.
