Evolution Version One

Humans are running the fittest software in the animal kingdom. We have the ability to plan, reason, and experience emotion with unmatched complexity and intensity. Why is this the case? One explanation is that the earliest ancestor of all life was itself a single self-replicating being and ever since, each species has emerged breathlessly from the last, minimally variant from its parents. 

For 4.1 billion years, the hardware-software package has been the only available option, without any chance of decoupling. This causes relatively advanced species to have one body with ten major organ systems; one brain activity tightly synced to a unitary metabolism, resulting in a corpus with split-second reactions, self-sustainability and a strong sensitivity to the immediate environment. 

Of course, these factory settings come with drawbacks. With all our eggs in one basket, we are intimately exposed to random dangers; a spinal cord severed for a microsecond induces terminal paralysis, or a momentary stroke will cause irreversible neurological damage and comatosis, imminent death, or both. We are never more than local beings, constrained by our single, linear experience.

If we zoom out further, we can see other collateral effects of this all-or-nothing evolutionary paradigm; all species experiences a relatively short and explosive generative phase that (1) writes-to-disk permanently, and (2) plateaus shortly upon arrival. Psychological experiences onset from early life memories will shape us, and our ability to re-learn our environment rapidly deteriorates. Brain plasticity is a luxury that slips out of our grasp before we even seem to register its advantages.

But let us not despair, for we posses an important power that separates quite distinctly from any creature we know. Biological evolution occurs over aeons, and so we rely on strength in numbers, memesis, and a strong reversion to the mean. With stories and memes, we opt to transmit news around the world to the rest of our species within hours: unthinkable to any other species on Earth.

The Many Ships of Theseus

The first version of life possess the life of Theseus’ ship: every timber, every rope, every nail gradually replaced over time, yet we intuitively retain our identity, our memories and ego, despite this process of constant metabolic destruction and regeneration. 

To extend this classical metaphor into territory relevant to technological immortality, let me present *The Many Ships of Theseus*. For spoilers (and those in a rush), please skip to the next chapter.

Weary from his famous adventures, the great hero Theseus returned home to Athens to discover his father, King Aegeus, on his deathbed. Kneeling beside the royal bed, he learned a truth that he had long been kept secret: after many years of lost heirs and miscarriages, the King had beseeched Poseidon for a son. As with all divine deals, he of course received more than he bargained for:

*“Poseidon gave me two treasures: you and a magnificent vessel - each masterfully crafted, unrivalled in form and function. But your fates are bound by immortal decree: should the ship sink, you too shall depart this world.”*

Theseus, stunned, placed his hand upon his father's and swore a solemn oath that he would dedicate his life to preserve the ship and keep it afloat. Yet his father delivered a final warning with his final words: 

*"Like all vessels, this one demands constant restoration. But your ship bears a curse: no matter what wealth I lavish upon it, no matter how gifted the carpenter, this ship ages with unnatural haste. Year by year, the fractures have deepened, and soon it will be damaged beyond salvation. The inevitable breach will draw you both to early graves.”*

And with that, the king’s eyes closed forever.

A little shaken, Theseus decided to venture on-board and see what he was working with. Climbing down under the hull he was alarmed to discover a labyrinth of confusing plumbing, a destructive furnace belching black smoke, and several obvious leaks threatening the integrity of the wood. Fortunately, he managed to drag the smoking machine onto the deck and push it overboard before it caused any lasting damage, then covered the smaller cracks with a thick layer of varnish.

Before long, Theseus transformed into a master shipwright, his hands becoming as skilled with adze and awl as they had once been with sword and shield. But as he sailed the Mediterranean, searching of ways to overcome Poseidon’s curse, he despaired to find with each restoration hairline fractures inexorably widen into threatening fissures, and malignant rot on fresh panels.

So Theseus abandoned his manuals, adopting experimental materials instead: in Phoenicia he bought resins to repel the teeth of barnacles, in Algiers he swapped his timbers for copper-tin alloys impervious to decay, and in Crimea he used ceramic plates to carry strain like no wood ever could. 

He sailed beyond the Mediterranean to distant shores: to Nordic fjords where master vikings used razor-thin hydrofoils to kissed the water’ surface instead of cutting through it; to Casablanca, where weavers spun sails of delicate silk that captured air too gentle for normal canvas to detect; and to Cork, where submersibles let sailors stay buoyant beneath the waves. 

As dawn broke over the Aegean, ten years after his father’s death, Theseus stood tall on the deck of *The Hydra*: his masterpiece of naval engineering. The vessel had become legend among seafarers. He sailed with confidence [*between Scylla and Charybdis](*https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Between_Scylla_and_Charybdis) and into Corinthian waters. Nothing, not even Poseidon, could sink *The Hydra*. 

Theseus squinted at the horizon. Two shadows had followed him through the strait, growing larger with each passing minute. He raised his looking glass and studied them: a pair of small Corinthian vessels with weathered hulls and heavy canvas sails, the kind that dotted every harbour from Cyprus to Gibraltar.

Theseus raised his looking glass to view the two ships that had followed him through the straight. Not warships at all—just weather-beaten scavenger boats with patched sails and timber hulls. He lowered the glass with a dismissive smile. These ragged vessels would present no challenge to his revolutionary masterpiece.

As Theseus watched their approach, the first whisper of doubt entered his mind. The scavengers moved with disturbing coordination. When *The Hydra* changed course, they mirrored his movements—one vessel to port, the other to starboard. Theseus activated his hydrofoils to gain speed, but the lead boat had already cut across his bow. Turning to face it, the second vessel struck from behind.

The first scavenger rammed *The Hydra*'s port side with crude iron-tipped horns. It ground through Theseus's ceramic plates, crushing the Crimean weldings. Water rushed into the breach. He sealed the compartment and spun the wheel hard, catching the breeze in his silk sails and aiming for the gap between his attackers.

They used crude grappling hooks to tear away sections of his custom-made rigging. From their boats, they hammered long chisels into the seams of his copper-tin plating, prying loose whole sections that crashed into the sea before being hauled aboard their vessels. They entangled his hydrofoils with chains, then wrenched them free with brute force, splintering the mounting points. They circled like sharks, darting in to steal another piece, then retreating with their prize.

That night, Theseus limped back to port, *The Hydra* battered but still afloat. He'd escaped the Corinthians by pure luck—a sudden squall had scattered them just as his vessel was taking on water. Twenty years of innovations had produced the most advanced ship in the Mediterranean, yet it remained catastrophically fragile. One attack. One storm. One reef. That's all it would take.

Theseus began the dismantling with methodical precision. The carbon-fiber bow became the core of three swift scout vessels. The deconstructed stern was split across five cargo ships. The hydrofoils were reincorporated a dozen skimmers that could outrun any pursuer. The submersion tanks became the heart of three vessels that could travel beneath the waves.

Within a year, Theseus's fleet dominated the Mediterranean, distributing and multiplying. Within a decade, *The Hydra* ventured into the Atlantic, the Red Sea and the Indian Ocean, some modified to withstand crushing Arctic ice, others to endure the scorching equator. Some were no longer recognisable as ships at all—becoming wheeled land vehicles, submersibles that could remain underwater for months, even primitive gliders that soared above mountains.

Poseidon watched from his underwater palace as *The Hydra* spread across every sea. The god had cursed a ship, not a network, and yet here he was. Poseidon soon lost track of all the extent of the Hydra; no storm or strike would reclaim his bargain. 

The Second Strain of Evolution

Having traversed the first strain of evolution (from inert hardware to the self-editing hardware-software bundle) we arrive at a logical step function where the true potential of life goes forth. Whereas Life 3.0 (in Tegmark’s taxonomy) grants editable hardware and software within a single embodiment, **Evolution Version Two** introduces a paradigm shift: horizontal scaling.

**From Monolithic to Modular**

- **Life 3.0** remains susceptible to a single-point-of-failure; your super-engineered “ship” can still sink once.
- **Life 4.0**, by contrast, decomposes identity into **distributed nodes**—each node a fully functional embodiment (droid, drone, virtual agent) running a slice of the same “mind-software.”

In the long-discussed journey from man to Supreme Being, we often frame this through a singular lens. Such a being is: Life 3.0: editable hardware, editable software

- Born from a single protocol, such as human conception
- Experiences everything from a singular perspective (vertical scaling)
- Individual death remains the ultimate terminus

This is where Tegmark’s framework needs extending, and where ours starts. The intention of this essay is to draw attention to the real unlock of our condition: tomorrow’s evolutionary advantage lies not in having the most resilient ship but in having the perfect armada. In seeking Life 3.0, we come closer to a more alien advancement that will change the face of civilisation irreversibly:

- Life 4.0: Multiple editable hardware bodies with global, editable software

Members of Life 4.0 we’ll call Superlinear / Superbeings:

- Distributed consciousness across multiple substrates (horizontal and vertical scaling)
- Experience becomes decentralised across multiple embodiments
- Only system-wide destruction constitutes death

Implications of Chalmers’ Extended Mind Hypothesis; technology becomes part of the cognitive system. Thought and memory can be distributed across external systems. If something actively participates in thinking, it should be considered part of the mind itself. 

The single-vessel approach involves upgrading from wooden hull to steel, adding engines, installing navigation systems, following the first evolutionary strain. We remain singular, albeit with enhanced capabilities, possibly [longevity escape velocity](https://www.oscarmoxon.com/longevity/2022/07/03/dont-die.html) if we can overcome rust entirely. We improve our singular body but maintain a unified consciousness with a single point of failure.

My argument is that the fitness landscape dramatically favours this second strain: turning your vessel into a navy. Natural evolution has done this organically with the neocortex and the strength of the combined thought. But this is the long term direction of technological beings, too, and we should be prepared for it. Distribute consciousness across a fleet where no single vessel contains the entirety of the self, but remains more unified than singular beings.

When this becomes an option, it trivialises strain one.

Any organism that persists must minimise [free energy](https://www.oscarmoxon.com/hierarchies/2024/10/24/emergence.html)—the surprise across its Markov blanket where self meets reality. Young organisms explore widely, mapping reality by discoery. As biological beings age, they strategically shrink their exposure as pathology renders novelty costly.

The ultimate being would work to both avoid deterioration and to maximise exploration. Parts could be renewed, expanded, or specialised without compromising the whole. Risks could be taken more ambitiously than ever before. This is why any first generation evolutionary being would lose out; they would not have the redundancy to persist in a world of distributed beings.

The Mechanics of Horcruxes

Lets differentiate the identity of such a being from other sci-fi and fantasy metaphors. 

The backup approach treats consciousness as data that can be stored and reinstantiated. Fiction like "Rick and Morty" explores this concept extensively—killing the protagonist repeatedly while a clone activates elsewhere with memories intact up to the point of backup.

This strategy fundamentally misses the mark. Making copies isn't evolutionarily advantageous if you only inhabit one vessel at a time. If Elon Musk died and a clone emerged later with new memories, society wouldn't transfer his assets or authority to this doppelgänger. Continuity between original and copy is integral.

Connected clones would, however, fit this description. Anything less requires communication to coordinate, introducing latency, divergence, and security vulnerabilities of evolution version one. They experience reality separately and incrementally drift apart, becoming a coalition rather than a unified intelligence.

Sleeves and Disks

The "sleeve" approach—popularized in "Altered Carbon"—treats bodies as modular equipment while consciousness remains singular. Your digital "disk" transfers between vessels but maintains unity of experience.

While offering improved adaptability, this preserves the catastrophic single-point-of-failure vulnerability. Destroying the disk (or whatever houses the consciousness) remains fatal. You've increased mobility but haven’t fundamentally altered the architecture of existence.

This is the biological equivalent of moving your brain between bodies—convenient, but still constrained by the binary nature of mortality. One failure and the entire system collapses.

Horcruxes

This provides our most apt metaphor. By fragmenting his soul across multiple vessels, Voldemort ensured that destroying any single container—or even most of them—couldn't eliminate his existence. As long as one fragment survived, he persisted.

But even this falls short of the full potential. Voldemort's fragments merely preserved his existence; they didn't enhance his agency. Better dark magic would have allowed him to actively perceive and operate through all vessels simultaneously—extending himself.

If each horcrux could be copied, paused, restored, run in parallel—or even *scaled,* this becomes an important consideration and the inevitable next evolutionary step: consciousness as network rather than node. Not sequential immortality but parallel existence. Not backing up or transferring but distributing.

The notion of a syntellect is a remote intellect, capable of computation with varying degrees of centrelines across varying numbers of bodies. Rather than human bodies, in the actual world this would materialise as droids (humanoids, subhumanoids, superhumanoids, robotics), vehicles of all kinds, drones, and . This synthetic, distributed machine-form is what gives syntellect their name. 

This distribution provides unprecedented advantages:

1. **Catastrophe resistance**: No single point of failure can terminate the system.
2. **Specialised processing**: Different cognitive functions run on optimised hardware.
3. **Parallel embodiment**: Simultaneous operation through multiple physical platforms.
4. **Graduated continuity**: Loss of components produces "phantom limb" phenomena rather than death.
5. **Evolutionary adaptability**: The system can incrementally upgrade or replace components without disrupting identity.

Machine consciousness already demonstrates this trajectory. When a neural network pauses and resumes operation with its weights and biases intact, it functionally continues as the same system despite the interruption. Extend this to a rich, multi-modal model with extensive experiential data, and you approach a system functionally indistinguishable from organic consciousness—yet immune to permanent termination unless all memories are destroyed. This is plug-and-playable life, and it’s extremely superior to one-shot.

Consciousness then becomes deconstructed: not a binary state but a gradient of properties across experiential richness, unified coherence, reality differentiation, memory sovereignty, and autonomous persistence. Remove any component and the conscious experience degrades accordingly. A slave lacks freedom of mind. A dementia patient lacks continuity. A sociopath lacks empathic capacity.

The optimal configuration distributes these properties across redundant systems while maintaining unified agency. You'd experience minimal cognitive damage because thought patterns would be backed up across multiple nodes. You might experience "phantom limb" phenomena when sections are damaged, but your core identity would persist and rebuild.

Beings will evolve into distributed cognitive assemblages with varying degrees of centralization. Each syntellect will comprise an array of cognitive horcruxes that are simultaneously controlled and semi-autonomous. This configuration allows beings to transcend death and become exponentially harder to eliminate.
